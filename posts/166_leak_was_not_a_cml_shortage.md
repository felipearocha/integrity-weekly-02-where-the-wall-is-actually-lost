# The Leak Was Not a Shortage of CMLs

Monday 2026-11-23

A circuit with 12 CMLs leaks. The corrective action says: increase to 30. I think that action fixes the report, not the circuit.

Do the arithmetic on a typical case. Sixty metres of 6 inch pipe has about 32 square metres of surface. A spot reading examines roughly the footprint of the probe, under one square centimetre. Twelve CMLs look at 0.003% of the pipe. Thirty look at 0.007%. If the leak came from a thin area the size of a hand, both numbers say the same thing: you were not there.

If the loss on that circuit is general, twelve points already told you what thirty will. If it is localized, eighteen more dots on straight runs and convenient elbows are eighteen more chances to read good wall.

What changes the odds is area, placed by mechanism. Four scans of 300 by 300 mm, at the fittings where that fluid actually takes metal, examine about 150 times more surface than the thirty spots, and it is the right surface.

API 570 5.6.3 puts the burden where it belongs: "Inspectors must use their knowledge (and that of others) of the process unit to optimize the CML allocation for each circuit, balancing the effort of collecting the data with the benefits provided by the data."

There is a cost on the other side too. Every CML that never moves still gets read, entered and reviewed. With two inspectors and tens of thousands of points, flat lines bury the three that matter. The same code lets you archive a CML and keep its history.

So after a leak I ask for three things: the mechanism, the locations it favours, and scans there. Then I ask which existing CMLs can go.

When did your program last retire a CML, and on what argument?

## Note

Reference: API 570, 4th Edition (2016), 5.6.3 CML Allocation; archiving inactive CMLs is described in 6.5.4. The arithmetic assumes a 10 mm probe footprint on 60 m of NPS 6 pipe (168.3 mm outside diameter).
